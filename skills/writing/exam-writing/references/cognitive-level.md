# Cognitive level: revised Bloom and scenarios

Numbers in brackets refer to [references.md](references.md).

## The revised taxonomy

Anderson and Krathwohl [8] revised Bloom's 1956 taxonomy in 2001. Krathwohl [9] summarizes the two changes that matter for writing exams:

1. The levels become verbs of cognitive process: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate and create.
2. A second dimension is added, the type of knowledge: factual, conceptual, procedural and metacognitive. Each objective sits in a 6 x 4 table.

| Process [9] | What the student does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Remember | Retrieves a fact | Name the three states of matter |
| Understand | Explains or classifies in their own words | Explain why ice floats on liquid water |
| Apply | Uses a procedure on a new case | Convert a recipe's quantities for a different number of servings |
| Analyze | Breaks something into parts and finds the cause | Read a lab notebook and point out which step made the result wrong |
| Evaluate | Judges against a criterion | Choose between two survey designs for a study with given constraints |
| Create | Produces something new | Design an experiment to test a given hypothesis |

## Higher-level multiple-choice items

Scully [10] argues, against the common assumption that higher-order thinking needs essay questions, that multiple-choice items can measure some higher-level skills if they are built for that purpose. Only the abstract of [10] was read. Guideline 3 of Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez [1] gives the mechanism, which is to use new material and paraphrase the language used in class, so that students can't recognize the answer from memory.

The NBME guide [2] asks that most items carry a scenario (vignette) and a closed question at the end. Its example with three versions of the same item shows the effect. The lower group (bottom 20%) got it right 90% of the time with no scenario, 82% with a short scenario and 66% with a long one. The upper group (top 20%) got it right 99% of the time with no scenario and 98% with a short one, and [2] reports that the long scenario wasn't much harder for them either. Adding the scenario lowered the lower group's success by 24 points and barely moved the upper group.

### Structure of a scenario

The NBME guide [2] orders a clinical scenario as presentation, history, findings, test results and question. Outside medicine, the same order becomes:

1. **Context.** Which system, which people, what changed.
2. **Evidence.** A data table, a measurement, an excerpt from a document, a number.
3. **Closed question.** For example "What is the most likely cause?", "Which change fixes it?", or "Which method would detect this error?".

The NBME rules for the stem still apply. The scenario holds all the data (the options add no information), the item can be answered with the options covered, and the question isn't negative.

### Before and after

Before (remember):
> Which measure of center is least affected by a single extreme value?
> A. Mean  B. Median  C. Midrange  D. Mode

After (apply or analyze):
> A street has 9 houses. Eight sold for between $180,000 and $220,000, and one sold for $2,400,000. A local paper wants to report one number for the typical house price on the street. Which measure should it report?
> A. The mean of the 9 prices
> B. The median of the 9 prices
> C. The midrange of the 9 prices
> D. The mean of the 9 prices rounded to the nearest $10,000

The "after" version doesn't name the property, so the student has to apply it.

Before (remember): "What is the definition of the median?"

After (analyze): show a table with the delivery times of 5 orders, and ask for the median delivery time and whether it meets a stated service target.

## Rules

- **Do:** write next to each item the verb from [9] that it measures. If every item says "remember", the exam measures memory even if the course objectives say "apply".
- **Do:** write scenarios with material the student didn't see word for word in class (guideline 3 of [1]), such as another domain, other numbers, or another data set.
- **Don't:** add a scenario to a recall item just to make it look like an application item. Guideline 16 of [1] forbids decorative text, so if the scenario doesn't change the reasoning, cut it.
- **Don't:** use long scenarios on every item. Each scenario costs reading time (guideline 13 of [1]). On a timed exam, save them for apply and analyze objectives.
