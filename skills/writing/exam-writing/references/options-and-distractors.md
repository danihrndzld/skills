# Number of options and writing distractors

Numbers in brackets refer to [references.md](references.md).

## What the evidence says about the number of options

Rodriguez [3] synthesized 80 years of studies on the number of options. His abstract concludes that with 3-option items you can give more questions per unit of time than with 4 or 5, you cover more content, and the psychometric quality of the scores does not get worse. Secondary summaries of the meta-analysis add two details, which come from those summaries and not from the original text. Going from 4 to 3 options was the only reduction that did not lower reliability, and removing a distractor at random does lower it, while removing the least chosen one does not.

Guideline 18 of Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez [1] sums it up as "write as many effective options as you can, but three are enough".

Tarrant, Ware and Mohammed [4] measured what happens in practice with 4-option items written by teachers. They analyzed 514 items from 7 nursing exams and counted as "non-functioning" any distractor chosen by fewer than 5% of examinees or with positive discrimination.

| Functioning distractors per item | Share of items [4] |
|---|---|
| 0 | 12.3% |
| 1 | 34.8% |
| 2 | 39.1% |
| 3 | 13.8% |

Only 52.2% of the distractors functioned, and 10.2% were chosen by nobody [4]. Adding up the first three rows, 6 out of 7 items worked with 2 effective distractors or fewer [4].

## What it means for a 4-option item

1. A weak fourth distractor adds nothing. If nobody picks it, the item behaves like a 3-option item, except that the student had to read one more line.
2. The chance of guessing right changes less than it seems. An absurd distractor already produces the increase in the table below without anyone noticing, because everyone rules it out.

   | Example: a block of 20 items worth 0.25 points each | 4 options | 3 options |
   |---|---|---|
   | Chance of a correct guess | 25% | 33% |
   | Expected score from pure guessing, out of 5 | 1.25 | 1.67 |

3. There's a practical reason to keep 4 options, which is to use the same answer sheet and the same scale across all versions. If you keep 4, the fourth distractor has to come from a real student error.

## Rules for the number of options

- **Do:** write the key and 2 distractors that come from observed errors first. Add a fourth only if a third real error exists.
- **Don't:** fill the fourth option with something nobody would choose (for example "A programming language from 1960" or "None, the planet has no atmosphere").
- **Do:** when a 4-option item had a distractor nobody chose on the last exam, remove that distractor and not another one at random [3].
- **Allowed:** mixing 3-option and 4-option items on the same exam. Guideline 18 of [1] doesn't require a fixed number.

Before (4 options, one filler):
> Which instrument measures air pressure?
> A. Barometer
> B. Thermometer
> C. Hygrometer
> D. A kitchen spoon

After (3 plausible options):
> A weather station records that the reading on one instrument dropped steadily for six hours before a storm. Which instrument was it?
> A. Barometer
> B. Hygrometer
> C. Thermometer

B and C stand for two plausible confusions, which are linking storms with humidity and linking storms with a drop in temperature.

## Writing distractors from real errors

Guideline 30 of [1] asks you to write distractors from students' typical errors, and guideline 29 asks that all of them be plausible. Guideline 28f forbids absurd options. The review by Gierl, Bulut, Guo and Zhang [5] notes that distractor writing has received much less research than the rest of the item, and it summarizes the available guidelines for writing and evaluating distractors.

Useful sources of real errors:

| Source | Example of an error that becomes a distractor |
|---|---|
| Wrong answers on earlier quizzes | The most chosen wrong option of a quiz |
| Graded homework | A step done in the right order with the wrong unit |
| Questions students asked in class | Mixing up two terms that sound alike |
| Wrong short answers on an earlier exam | The most repeated wrong explanation, copied almost word for word |

- **Do:** write each distractor as the answer a student with a specific error would give, and note which error it stands for. If you can't name the error, the distractor is filler.
- **Don't:** write a distractor that is true in another context unless the stem rules that context out. That produces two keys.

Before:
> Why did the plant in the experiment die?
> A. Because it got no light. (key)
> B. Because plants are fragile.
> C. Because something went wrong.
> D. Because of bad luck.

After:
> A plant was kept in a closed dark cupboard for three weeks. It was watered on schedule, and the soil and temperature were the same as for a control plant on a windowsill, which stayed healthy. What most likely caused the plant in the cupboard to die?
> A. It could not carry out photosynthesis without light. (key)
> B. It got too much water because the soil did not dry out in the dark.
> C. It ran out of oxygen inside the closed cupboard.
> D. It used up the nutrients in the soil faster in the dark.

B, C and D are examples of errors a student might make. On a real exam, each one should be an error you have seen in your own students, and you should note it next to the option.
