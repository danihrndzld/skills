# Matching items

Numbers in brackets refer to [references.md](references.md).

## State of the evidence

Matching has far less experimental research than multiple choice. No verified study was found that measures the effect of homogeneity, the number of extra responses, or order in a matching item. The rules below come from two verified sources that treat matching as a family of multiple-choice items sharing one list of options:

- Guideline 9 of Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez [1] includes matching among the recommended formats, and the guidelines on options (homogeneity, logical order, plausibility) apply to the list of responses.
- The NBME guide [2] describes the extended matching format (type R, sets of 5 to 20 items with a single list of options) and the retired type B format. About type B it notes that, because each option could be used once, several times or not at all, it couldn't be solved by elimination, and that its flaw was the lack of a lead-in question, which made the task ambiguous.

Course materials often recommend a maximum number of premises (6 or 7, or 10 to 15, depending on the source). Those numbers could not be traced to a primary source and are not cited here.

## Terms

- **Premises** are the left column, what is being asked.
- **Responses** (or options) are the right column, what is chosen.

## Rules

**Rule 1. Homogeneous lists.** All premises are the same kind of thing and all responses are too (guideline 23 of [1], and option homogeneity in [2]). If the premises are types of rock, every response is a description of how a rock forms, not a mix of minerals, tools and rock types. A mixed list lets students rule options out by category.

Before:
> Premises: 1. Igneous 2. Sedimentary 3. Hammer 4. Quartz
> Responses: A. A tool for splitting rock. B. A common mineral. C. Formed from cooled molten rock. D. Formed from layers of compressed particles. E to G. ...

With this list, the hammer and quartz are matched by category without knowing anything about rock formation.

After:
> Premises: 1. Igneous 2. Sedimentary 3. Metamorphic 4. Volcanic glass
> Responses: seven descriptions of how a rock forms, all of the same length and structure.

**Rule 2. More responses than premises.** With 4 premises and 4 responses, the last one is answered by elimination. For example, 4 premises and 7 responses leave 3 extra. The extra responses are distractors and must be plausible (guideline 29 of [1]). Each one describes a real error, for example a description of sedimentary rock that confuses compression with melting.

**Rule 3. Instructions that state the basis of the match.** Say what is matched with what and whether a response can be used more than once. The flaw [2] points out in type B, the missing lead-in, is fixed with one line:

> "For each rock type (1 to 4), write the letter of the description that matches it. Each letter is used at most once; 3 are left over."

**Rule 4. Logical order in the response list.** Guideline 21 of [1]: alphabetical, numeric or chronological. Students then find the answer without scanning the whole list for each premise, and the order doesn't give the key away.

**Rule 5. Everything on the same page.** If the response list is on the next page, students turn the page for every premise, which adds copying errors that don't measure content (irrelevant difficulty, in the terms of [2]).

**Rule 6. Short responses and long premises, not the other way round.** Students read each premise once and scan the response list several times (once per premise), so whatever gets reread should be the shortest. This rule applies guideline 13 of [1] (minimize reading).

**Rule 7. Decouple the order of premises and responses.** If premise 1 matches A, 2 matches B and so on, a student who matches in order gets everything right. The same applies to a diagram with numbered panels whose order follows the list of names.

## Scoring

Give each premise the same points and score each premise on its own. A mistake on premise 1 shouldn't carry over to the others, even if the response it used is no longer available.
